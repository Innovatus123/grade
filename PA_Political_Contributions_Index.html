Plate 01 — Position summary

Two findings drive everything else. First, one individual — Jeffrey Yass — is the ultimate source of $71.25M reaching Pennsylvania politicians across 17 vehicles and up to 5 hops of PAC-to-PAC transfer, against $33.79M in direct giving. Second, building trades labor outspends construction contracting roughly 10 to 1 ($29.7M vs $3.1M). If GBCA members are outspent by an order of magnitude on the labor side of their own industry, that is a board-level fact.

Why the organizational / individual split is the primary axis

Pennsylvania bars corporations from contributing directly to candidates (25 P.S. § 3253). Corporate money must move through a separate segregated fund, so at state level it almost never looks corporate — it arrives as PAC money. That is why this sheet carries a sponsor-tracing layer: without it, corporate influence is structurally invisible.

The construction read. Building trades labor gave $29.7M; construction contracting gave $3.1M. Roughly ten to one, inside GBCA's own industry.

Plate 05 — Following money through the PAC network

Most large Pennsylvania PACs are non-connected — they have no corporate sponsor by design, so “who sponsors it” is the wrong question. The right one is who funds them, and because those PACs are themselves recipients in this dataset, the answer was already in the data. Where one funder supplies 50% or more of a PAC's receipts, that becomes a directed edge and the chain is walked to its origin.

Plate 06 — Method, and what not to trust

Sources

PA Department of State full campaign finance export · City of Philadelphia campfin_contributions · FEC bulk files filtered to Pennsylvania. 3,157,741 rows ingested, 3,014,777 after removing cross-jurisdiction duplicates, 1,287 reporting rollup lines excluded.

Four things to know before quoting any number

1. All donor classification is derived. No jurisdiction publishes a reliable corporate-versus-individual flag. PA has no such field at all; Philadelphia's is 92% “Not Specified”; only the FEC's entity code is dependable. Every row carries the method and a confidence score.

2. Totals are taken on primary rows only. 47% of Philadelphia dollars duplicate a PA state row for committees that file in both places. Duplicates are flagged, not deleted.

3. Entities are deliberately under-merged. A wrong merge produces a fabricated influence claim, so borderline matches go to a review queue instead. A donor's resolved total is a floor, not a ceiling.

4. Chains use a 50% dominance threshold. A PAC funded 45/45/10 by three sources has no chain edge and reads as its own origin.

Defects found by auditing output

Every meaningful error in this build was caught by checking results, not by reasoning about the design. Four Williamses merged into one donor. A Reading PA “John M. Arnold” folded into the Houston megadonor. Comma-stripping reversed every federal name, so FITZPATRICK, BRIAN parsed as first-name Fitzpatrick and federal records never matched their PA counterparts. Regexes ending in a word boundary meant CARPENTER never matched “CARPENTERS”, and building trades money read $8.1M instead of $29.7M. The FEC's connected-organization field is free text for some filers and attributed Commonwealth Leaders Fund to a sponsor named “DEATON” — a $16M misattribution.
